Daily routine. Read 1.5–2 hrs (the Book at doc.rust-lang.org/book, Rust by Example) · Exercises 2–3 hrs (Rustlings, Exercism) · Build 2–3 hrs (the week's project) · Review 30 min (write down 3 things you got wrong or learned). Install Rust with rustup on Day 1, use rust-analyzer in VS Code, enable clippy and rustfmt, and type every line yourself.

Week 1: Core language

Ownership is the hard part. Spend the time here.

Week 2: Real-world essentials

Collections, errors, generics, traits, lifetimes.

Week 3: Intermediate features

Testing, closures, iterators, smart pointers.

Week 4: Concurrency and a real project

Threads, dependencies, then your capstone.

Rules that make this work

  • Never copy-paste solutions. Read the error, try twice, then search.
  • Don't skip ownership and lifetimes days. They are why Rust feels hard.
  • Take real breaks. After 6–8 hours quality drops; split study into morning and evening.
  • Keep a "compiler errors I met" file. Reviewing it is one of the fastest ways to improve.
  • Be realistic. After 30 days you are early intermediate: comfortable with the language and common tools, but slower than someone with six months of experience. The rest comes from projects.
After Day 30: learn async (tokio), unsafe and FFI, macros, advanced types, and performance profiling. Read Rust for Rustaceans, the Rustonomicon, and the Async Book, then contribute to open-source crates.
Resources: The Rust Programming Language (doc.rust-lang.org/book), Rustlings, Rust by Example, Exercism Rust track, Jon Gjengset's YouTube videos.
